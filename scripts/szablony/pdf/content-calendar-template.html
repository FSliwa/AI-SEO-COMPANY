Content calendar template: 3-month plan

An SEO content plan for your blog and website. Each row is one piece of content with a primary keyword, search intent, format, target URL, internal links, an owner and results from Google Search Console at 30 and 90 days (plus GA4 inquiries at 90 days).

How to use this template

  1. Set the basics (part 1): the goal for the quarter, the audience, the service pages to support and a cadence your team can actually keep. Take topics and target pages from your SEO strategy if you have one.
  2. Build the topic backlog (part 2) from Search Console, keyword research, Google autocomplete and People also ask, customer questions and competitor gaps. Rate each topic from 1 to 3 on three scales and work out the score.
  3. Give each topic one primary keyword and search it on Google to see which pages rank in the top ten. That tells you the intent and the format. Keywords that share at least 4 of their top 10 results belong on one URL.
  4. Lay out the calendar (part 3): month 1 – service pages and FAQs, month 2 – articles and comparisons, month 3 – case studies and updates.
  5. Run the checklist before publishing (part 5), and on publish day add the 30-day and 90-day check dates.
  6. Measure and decide (part 4): filter the Search Console Performance report by URL, add GA4 inquiries at 90 days and log your decision. Review the quarter at the end (part 6).

Where to find topics

  • Search Console: Performance report, Queries tab, sorted by impressions. Queries with impressions but few clicks: positions 4–20 mean expanding a page you have, lower positions with no dedicated page mean a new topic.
  • Keyword research: how customers describe your services, what they ask and in which months they search. Keywords that share at least 4 of their top 10 results = one topic and one URL (our working rule).
  • Google autocomplete and People also ask: your customers' exact wording. A long query usually becomes a heading or an FAQ question; it gets its own page only if the intent is different.
  • Customer questions: from calls, emails and sales meetings. A question that keeps coming up is a topic for an FAQ or an article.
  • Competitor gaps: topics that businesses ranking above you cover and you don't.

5. Pre-publish checklist

  • The primary keyword is in the page title, the H1 and the first paragraph. The title is about 50–60 characters: Google has no limit, but usually truncates longer titles.
  • The content matches the intent you see in Google's top ten results.
  • The piece adds something the top ten don't have: your own data, an example, project photos, an expert's view.
  • Under each question heading, the first one or two sentences answer directly, and numbers have a source.
  • The author, date and sources are visible on the page.
  • No other URL on the site targets the same keyword.
  • At least two existing pages link to the new piece, and it links to the right service page and 2–4 related pieces on the same topic.
  • The meta description says what the reader will find and gives a reason to click.
  • Any text drafted with AI has been fact-checked and edited by a person.
  • The publish date is in the calendar, and the 30-day and 90-day check dates are in the team calendar.
  • In GA4, form submissions and clicks on your phone number are marked as key events (GA4 does not count them retroactively).
  • For updates: the "last updated" date changes only when the content has really changed.
